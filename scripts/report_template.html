Drex Vertical Reframe

Turn long landscape video into vertical shorts, automatically. Find the moments worth watching, decide frame by frame which part of the picture to keep, then stitch those decisions into steady camera work. The same method now works on two kinds of content.

Why it matters

Shot for widescreen. Watched vertically.

Creators publish long-form video on YouTube in 16:9, but short-form reach lives in the 9:16 feeds of TikTok, Reels, and Shorts. Repurposing existing footage is the cheapest source of new content, but you can’t just repost it.

Football: the camera follows the ball

→
The problem

Sports content teams sit on hours of full-match landscape replays, while their audience scrolls vertically on TikTok, Douyin, and Reels. Turning a 90-minute match into vertical highlights today means an editor picking moments and dragging a crop frame by hand.

The hard part: vertical keeps only about a third of the landscape width. Which third depends on what’s happening: follow the ball on the attack, the goal on a shot, the players celebrating after.

  • Find the moments: pull goals, red cards, and winners out of the full match.
  • Pick the frame: decide, every frame, which part to keep.
  • Keep it steady: no jitter within a shot, ball never leaves the frame.
The approach

Take a full landscape football broadcast and cut it into phone-ready vertical highlights automatically: find the moments worth watching, like goals and red cards, decide every frame which part of the picture to keep, and move the camera smoothly with the ball, like a human operator would.

Crop logic

Where each frame’s crop comes from

  1. Drex reads the grid. Every 0.5s we take a frame, overlay vertical lines 0–16 and horizontal lines A–J, and ask Drex six questions: the left, right, top, and bottom lines of the region to keep, plus the vertical and horizontal lines the ball or face sits on. Every answer comes with a probability distribution; the tighter it is, the more confident that frame.
  2. Convert to a 9:16 box. Use the smallest 9:16 box that contains the region. When the region is too wide for vertical (common in wide shots), center on the ball or face instead.
  3. Smooth within each shot. Each shot gets its own smooth camera path that stays close to Drex’s calls (high-confidence frames weigh more) without accelerating too hard, with a 0.8s time constant. Shot boundaries are hard cuts.
  4. The ball must stay in frame. After smoothing, check every frame: if the ball drifts outside the middle 80% of the crop, pin a hard constraint at that frame to pull the box back and re-solve, up to 3 rounds.

Comparison

Against the alternatives: quality, cost, latency

The Gemini native-video baseline initially picked its own time ranges, and 2 of its 3 timestamps were off by about 9 minutes, so comparing crops directly would compare different footage. We split it in two: first “finding moments,” then “picking the frame” on the same clips Drex cut, with all three approaches sharing the same smoothing and rendering code.

Finding moments: can it locate the 4 key events in 30 minutes, on time?

Picking the frame: same clips, whose camera keeps up with the ball?

There’s no human ground truth, so “ball in frame” is computed twice, once against each model’s own ball positions. Scoring well only against your own reference may just mean self-consistency; scoring high on both is more credible. Cost covers model calls only: Drex Max v1.1 bills 30 nUSD per input token ($0.03 per million) with free output; Gemini is estimated at list price ($1.50 per million input, $7.50 per million output and thinking). Local transcription, shot detection, and rendering are excluded. Drex uses 36× more input tokens but costs 50× less per token, so each clip is still about 20× cheaper.

Known issues

Still open

  • Drex misreads fast-moving close-ups. For example, at 0:16 in the 01:21:25 clip, Rakitić runs off to celebrate and Drex places his face about 200px left of where it is, so the crop drifts with it. The longer the bar on a red dot in the path chart, the less sure Drex is. Next: fall back to a lightweight face detector on close-ups or high uncertainty; in wide shots, locate coarsely first, then zoom in and ask for the ball again.
  • The same goal gets clipped twice. Replays and celebrations after a goal are also classified as “goal.” Next: dedupe same-type events within 90 seconds, or treat replays as an extension of the same event.
  • We can’t yet say whether it really finds the ball in wide shots. The grid test has no human ground truth, only eyeballed overlays. Accuracy needs labels from tools/label.html.
  • Recall keywords were tuned on this match. Before moving to Belgium vs. Japan, validate on that match to avoid overfitting.
  • Transcription used local Whisper. The venue firewall blocked NDI at the time; once the network allows it, switch back to NDI and compare.
Scenario 2 · Three-person interview show · 30 min · edited multi-camera footage

Interview: the camera follows the speaker

→
The problem

Podcasts, interviews, and keynotes usually reach vertical platforms as 30–60 second takes. An editor has to read the whole thing to find passages that stand on their own, then frame the speaker shot by shot.

The hard part: with two or three people in frame, vertical fits only one. Who to show depends on who’s talking, and the speaker often isn’t on screen.

  • Find the takes: pull self-contained points or stories from a 30-minute conversation, without starting or ending mid-sentence.
  • Follow the speaker: frame whoever is talking; when they’re off screen, frame the most relevant listener.
  • Cut, don’t pan: if people sit still, so should the camera; cut to the new speaker instead of sweeping across.

Camera stability

From “smooth follow” to “locked shot”

v1 reused football’s smooth follow: Drex’s position every 0.5s jitters by tens of pixels, so following it turned into drift, even though the people barely move. v2 takes one position per shot (the weighted median of Drex’s calls) and uses hard cuts instead of pans when it needs to move.

Totals across the 5 clips. “Face in frame” uses Drex’s own face positions as reference; no human labels.

Known issues

Still open

  • Some clips start mid-sentence. NDI segments are coarse (median ~17s), so we split sentences with local Whisper, but this time Whisper output almost no punctuation, the “sentences” came out incomplete, and Drex had to pick among them. Next: re-split with word-level timestamps and a larger model.
  • Changes shorter than 2s don’t trigger a cut. To avoid cutting too often, we cut only after the face is out of frame for 2+ seconds, so the first 1–2 seconds after a speaker change may stay on the previous person. Locking shots dropped face-in-frame from 99% to 97% in exchange for zero in-shot panning.
  • Speaker following was spot-checked on only 2 clips. No human labels; judged by eye from the debug videos.
  • Virtual cuts are unvalidated. They’re designed for single-camera, unedited footage, which this isn’t; testing needs a single-camera two-person conversation.
